# Evolution of Stone Tools

Stone tools are among the oldest artifacts, dating back at least 2.6 million years.

## Lithic Artifacts
- **Definition:** Lithic tools are stone artifacts, imperishable and commonly found at archaeological sites.
- **Oldest Artifacts:** Earliest lithic artifacts are butchering tools made by early human ancestors.
- **Age:** Tools date back at least 2.6 million years (Sahle & Gossa, 2019).

Lithic artifacts represent the earliest evidence of tool use by humans.

---

## Oldowan Tradition
- **Timeframe:** Oldowan tools date from 2.6 to 1.5 million years ago.
- **Location:** Named after Olduvai Gorge, Tanzania (Sahle and Gossa, 2019).
- **Characteristics:** Tools include minimally flaked stone cobbles (choppers) and unmodified cobbles.
- **Usage:** Used for cutting and scraping animal and plant materials (Lemorini et al., 2014).
- **Hominins:** Possible makers include Australopithecus africanus, Australopithecus garhi, Homo habilis, and Homo erectus/ergaster.

The Oldowan tradition marks the beginning of stone tool use by hominins.

---

## Acheulian Tradition
- **Timeframe:** Developed around 1.76 million years ago (Lepre et al., 2011).
- **Location:** Named after Saint-Acheul, France.
- **Characteristics:** Distinctive large bifaces (handaxes), scrapers, choppers, and cleavers.
- **Design:** Handaxes are teardrop-shaped, symmetrical, and sharp on all edges.
- **Usage:** Used for butchering and other purposes (Zupancich et al., 2018).
- **Hominins:** Likely made by Homo erectus/ergaster, with possible contributions from later species.

The Acheulian tradition represents a significant advancement in tool-making techniques.

---

## Mousterian Tradition
- **Timeframe:** Developed around 250,000 years ago, waned around 40,000 years ago.
- **Location:** Found throughout Europe and western Asia, associated with Neanderthals (Biagi and Starnini, 2014).
- **Characteristics:** Tools made using the Levallois technique, producing flakes of predetermined size and shape.
- **Flexibility:** Considered a flexible toolkit due to variability in tool types (Turq et al., 2013).
- **Extinction:** Coincided with the extinction of Neanderthals and the rise of Homo sapiens.

The Mousterian tradition showcases refined tool-making skills among Neanderthals.

---

## Types of Stone Tools
- **Chipped-Stone Tools:** Made from chert or obsidian, created by striking a core to produce flakes.
  - **Usage:** Flakes can be used as tools or further modified; cores can be shaped into tools.
  - **Examples:** Recognizable tools include spear or arrow points.
  
- **Groundstone Tools:** Made from various rock types, shaped by pecking or grinding.
  - **Usage:** Used for grinding seeds, nuts, and minerals; also shaped into axes and ornaments.
  - **Common Tools:** Handstone and lapstone used together for grinding seeds or maize.

Understanding the types of stone tools reveals the diverse methods of early human technology.

- **Influencing Factors:** 
  - AT/GC bias
  - Mutational pressure
  - Natural selection
- **Evolutionary Significance:** Codon usage bias impacts cellular mechanisms, gene expression, and evolutionary rates.
- **Parameters for Study:** Codon bias index (CBI), effective number of codons (ENC), codon adaptation index (CAI), and others.
- **Research Gaps:** Limited studies on Y-linked genes' codon usage patterns compared to nuclear genes.

# DSM-IV Criteria for Substance Abuse and Dependence

The DSM-IV criteria for substance use disorders present significant diagnostic challenges.

## Overview
- **Dependence Diagnosis:** Requires three or more criteria met.  
- **Abuse Diagnosis:** Requires at least one criterion met without dependence.  
- **Conceptual Framework:** Distinction between dependence syndrome and social consequences of heavy use.  
- **Hierarchy:** Dependence is prioritized over abuse in diagnosis.  
  
This section outlines the basic diagnostic criteria and their hierarchical structure.

---

## Strengths of DSM-IV
- **Reliability:** Dependence diagnosis is highly reliable.  
- **Validation:** Supported by treatment utilization, impaired functioning, and comorbidity indicators.  

The DSM-IV shows strong reliability and validation for dependence diagnosis.

---

## Problems with DSM-IV Approach
- **Lower Reliability:** Abuse diagnosis has lower reliability and validity.  
- **Single Criterion Issue:** Nearly half of abuse cases are diagnosed with only one criterion, often hazardous use.  
- **Severity Misconceptions:** Some abuse criteria indicate severe problems, contradicting milder assumptions.  
- **Incorrect Assumptions:** Studies show abuse is not merely a precursor to dependence; not all dependence cases meet abuse criteria.  

This section highlights significant issues with the reliability and assumptions of the DSM-IV approach.

---

## Hierarchical Issues
- **Diagnostic Orphans:** Cases with two dependence criteria but no abuse criteria lack a diagnosis despite potential severity.  
- **Improved Reliability:** Analyzing abuse criteria without dependence improves test-retest reliability.  
- **Factor Analysis Findings:** Criteria may form one or two highly correlated factors, suggesting a need for combination.  

Hierarchical issues in diagnosis can lead to overlooked cases and suggest a need for improved reliability.

---

## Item Response Theory Analysis
- **Purpose:** Investigate the relationship between abuse and dependence criteria.  
- **Criterion Severity:** Severity is inversely related to frequency; rarely endorsed criteria are more severe.  
- **Discrimination:** Better discrimination indicated by steeper slopes in graphical representations.  

This analysis explores the relationship between abuse and dependence criteria through item response theory.

---

## Findings from Item Response Theory Studies
- **Unidimensionality:** All DSM-IV criteria for abuse and dependence (except legal problems) indicate the same underlying condition.  
- **Severity Rankings:** Abuse and dependence criteria intermixed across severity spectrum in various studies.  
- **Evidence for Change:** Findings support removing the distinction between abuse and dependence in DSM-5.  

The findings suggest a reevaluation of the distinction between abuse and dependence in future classifications.

---

## Cultural and Gender Bias Examination
- **Prevalence Variability:** Substance use attitudes vary across demographics and cultures.  
- **Differential Item Functioning:** Most criteria did not show consistent bias across studies.  
- **Need for Further Research:** Additional research is suggested to explore potential biases in DSM-5 criteria.  

Cultural and gender biases in substance use attitudes highlight the need for further research in DSM-5 criteria.

---

The analysis of DSM-IV and subsequent findings indicate a significant need for reevaluation of substance use disorder classifications in DSM-5.
